The Business

A staffing firm with a
uniquely layered financial model.

The staffing firm operates as a workforce intermediary — placing skilled professionals with client companies on demand. When a company needs a resource, the firm provides one. The client pays the firm; the firm pays the worker. Crucially, when a placed employee leaves, the firm bears the responsibility of finding a replacement — removing hiring risk entirely for clients.

This creates a layered financial structure: billing cycles, placement fees, salary disbursements, outstanding balances, and conversion metrics all need to be tracked simultaneously across multiple client relationships. Understanding this model was foundational to designing a dashboard that made sense of the complexity — rather than just reflecting it.

Why this matters
The business model generates data from two directions at once — what clients owe, and what workers are owed. A dashboard that conflated these perspectives would serve no one.
The Problem

70–80 KPIs tracked.
No one could answer
where we're underperforming.

Despite tracking a large volume of metrics, leadership couldn't answer one question: where exactly is the business falling short? The existing Power BI dashboard had been built — but was effectively abandoned. Data wasn't fed into it regularly. Reports lived in scattered Excel files. Personas with overlapping information needs manually reconciled data across multiple sources before they could even begin analysis.

Core insight
Users had access to more data than most businesses could ask for — and still couldn't identify areas of underperformance. The signal was buried under the noise of manual processes and fragmented tooling.
Research

Five interviews.
Five distinct mental models.

Five in-depth interviews, each representing a unique organizational persona. The goal wasn't to validate assumptions — it was to map each persona's specific information needs, their current workarounds, and their decision-making triggers.

What emerged was overlapping — but contextually distinct — information needs. The SVP of Operations needed a bird's-eye view of the same metrics the Controller tracked in depth. The VP of Payroll and VP of Client Operations both needed billing data, but from opposite ends of the same transaction. These overlaps became the central design tension to resolve.

Research finding
Overlapping metrics across personas didn't mean the same dashboard served everyone. Context and level of detail made each persona's need fundamentally different.
Design Challenges

Three hard problems
the research surfaced.

01
Conflicting needs, overlapping metrics
Multiple personas required the same metrics but at different levels of granularity. Determining where a shared metric lived — without duplicating it or creating dead ends — required IA decisions grounded in actual job workflows, not just data structure.
02
One dashboard, three distinct audiences
Designing a single navigable product for leadership, financial, and operational personas without alienating any group. 70–80 KPIs needed a clear hierarchy so each persona could reach relevant information in the fewest steps — while the platform's consumption-only constraints ruled out any workflow shortcuts.
03
Visual density without cognitive overload
An analytical dashboard requires dense visual encoding — charts, tables, trend lines, comparisons. The challenge was progressive layering: summary-level insights first, depth on demand. Every chart type also had to be a deliberate UX decision, not a default — the right encoding for the cognitive task, within Power BI's rendering constraints.
The Solution

One product. Three domains.
One executive view.

The final solution is a Power BI dashboard built around a central navigation model: an executive summary screen feeding into three domain-specific screens. This architecture directly mirrors the organizational structure of the business — and the mental models of the five personas interviewed.

Financial Performance
Gross billing, total revenue, end profit — sliced across clients, time periods, and branches
Financial Operations
Employee count, hours billed, salary paid, outstanding balance, overdue tracking
Operational Performance
Client spend, success rates, headcount, conversion rates, business health metrics

The executive summary screen serves SVP-level personas — offering a quick read across all three domains so leadership can orient in under a minute. Domain-specific screens then provide the depth required by VP-level and Controller personas who need to diagnose, not just monitor.

UX Strategy

Where design decisions
shaped the architecture.

Information Architecture
Domain bucketing over persona bucketing
Rather than organizing by persona — which would have caused heavy duplication — we organized by business domain and used the executive summary as the cross-domain layer. Shared metrics surfaced in the summary; persona-specific depth lived in the domain screens. This eliminated redundancy while serving each user's actual workflow.
Visual Hierarchy
Progressive disclosure to manage density
With 70–80 KPIs to surface, we applied a strict hierarchy: summary metrics at the top, trend analysis in the middle, granular breakdowns at the bottom. At any zoom level, the user sees the most decision-relevant information first — not a wall of equally-weighted charts.
Adoption Strategy
Anchoring in existing mental models
The dashboard teardown phase was strategic — by understanding what metrics users already tracked, we preserved familiar language and metric definitions while restructuring how they were surfaced. This reduced adoption friction: the tool felt immediately legible, even as its architecture was new.
Visual Encoding
Chart type as a UX decision
Given the purely analytical nature of the product, chart selection was treated as a UX decision, not a styling one. Each type was chosen to match the cognitive task — trend charts for temporal analysis, bar charts for comparison, tables for precise operational records — ensuring the visualization served the insight.

What I carried forward from this project
The most important lesson was that information architecture is a strategic act, not a taxonomic one. How you bucket information determines what users can perceive — and in a business context, perception shapes decisions. Organizing 70–80 KPIs into three coherent domains wasn't just a navigation choice; it was a statement about how this company should think about its own performance.

The second lesson: the existing system matters as much as the ideal one. By tearing down the previous dashboard before designing the new one, we avoided repeating its structural mistakes while preserving the mental models users had already built. Designing for analysis is fundamentally different from designing for action — in a consumption-first product, every visual is a hypothesis, and the designer's job is to make sure those hypotheses are the right ones to ask.
